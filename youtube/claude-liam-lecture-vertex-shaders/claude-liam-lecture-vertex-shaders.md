## claude-liam-lecture-vertex-shaders-slate

Vertex shaders learn how a shader moves the corner points of a shape and how to give a shape enough corners to bend. By the end you can build a waving flag. Hello, this is Liam in Forbear. A waving flag looks as if thousands of pixels are moving. They are not. A vertex shader does not change pixels. It moves the corner points of a shape and the graphics card fills in everything between them. Five terms, a shader is a small program the graphics card runs to decide how something is drawn. A pixel is one dot of the picture. A vertex is a corner point of a shape. A polygon is a shape made of straight edges between points. To interpolate is to blend smoothly between two known values. This flag is waving and not one pixel in it was told to move. One line of code moves its corner points and the graphics card fills in everything between them. That line lives in a function called vertex. A shape is built from corner points, its vertices. The engine calls vertex once for each one and asks a single question, where does this corner go? The engine hands you the corner's position in a built-in, a value it gives your function. Its name is vertex two in capital letters. Whatever you leave in it is where the corner goes. It counts in pixels from the middle of the sprite. A sprite 128 pixels wide has corners at minus 64 and 64. The y axis points down so the top corners are the minus ones. Change the number and the corner moves, add 40 to x and every corner moves 40 pixels to the right. The whole square slides because the graphics card fills in the shape between the moved corners. The 40 is in the node's own pixels, counted before the node is scaled. Here the right hand square is scaled to twice its size, so the same 40 carries at 80 pixels across the screen. Multiply in the corners moved toward or away from the middle. Here one and a half times wider and half is tall. The square stretches and flattens. Still only four corners are moving. To treat corners differently tell them apart. The engine gives each corner an address two called uv, 0 to 1 across, 0 to 1 down. Top left is 0, 0. Bottom right is 1, 1. Now scale a move by the address, add 50 times the vertical address to x. The top corners add nothing because their address is 0. The bottom corners add 50, the square lanes. Only four corners moved in between the card fills the shape and even steps. We measured the left edge, 50 pixels over 128 rows on a straight line to within half a pixel. Remember that straight line. A slide a squash a lane, four corners are enough when the whole shape moves as one. Abend is different. Watch what a ripple does to a flag with four corners. A ripple is the sign of the address across, scaled and added to y. On this flag nothing ripples. The right end drops and every edge stays straight. Why vertex can only move the vertices that exist between two corners the card draws a straight line. A curve needs corners along the way for vertex to push up and down. So give the shape more vertices. Here are two ways in godo. A polygon node where you place the points yourself or a mesh node which draws a mesh, the vertices and triangles that make a shape. A mesh instance 2D node draws a mesh. Give it a quad mesh, a flat rectangle you can cut into a grid. Subdivide width and subdivide depth say how many extra cuts to make a cross and down. Count them cut a quad mesh 3 by 3 and each side gets five corner points, 25 vertices, always two more than the cuts each way. The flags 31 by 7 makes 33 by 9, 297. Same ripple, same picture. On the left four vertices, a tilt. On the right, 297, a ripple. Nothing in the shader changed only how many corners it had to work with. More vertices a smoother wave, four pieces show every joint, 10 soften them, 32 hide them. A rule of thumb, eight pieces per wave leaves the worst gap near 8% of the wave's height, 16 leaves about 2. Cut only where it bends. This ripple runs along the flag, never up and down, so rows of vertices add nothing. 31 cuts across and none down gives 66 vertices and the picture matches pixel for pixel. A polygon node gives you one vertex per point you place, with points only around the edge, godo fills the inside with long thin triangles and the picture twists. A grid of points joined into cells fixes it. Now let it travel. Time is seconds on one clock shared by the whole game, subtracted inside the sign and the ripple slides to the right as the seconds pass. But a flag is held at one end, multiply the ripple by the address across. At the left edge, the address is zero, so nothing is added and that edge stays put like a pole. Three numbers shape the wave, how tall, how many waves fit, how fast, make each a uniform, a value your game sets from outside the shader the same for every vertex and pixel. Now the flag has three dials. Raise amplitude and the waves get taller, raise frequency and more waves fit across, raise speed and they travel faster. Each one changes only what its name says. Your game can turn the dials while it runs. When the wind picks up, call set shader parameter on the node's material with the dial's name and a new value. The flag answers it once. Read it as one three dials at the top. In vertex the wave, the address across minus time through a sign scaled by the dials pinned by the address. Nine lines, one of them moving the flag. The flag moves but it looks like a flat sheet, nothing shades the folds. Shading is a job for fragment, the function that colors each pixel the shape covers, but fragment runs later and it never sees the corners. The way across is a varying. In vertex you set a value at each corner. In fragment you read it. The graphics card interpolates it across the shape and it flows one way forward. Four colors, one per corner, set in vertex. Fragment only reads them. The result is a smooth blend and an exact one. Halfway along the top edge, red reads one half the average of its two corners. Switch the blending off with flat and each triangle keeps one corners value. A mesh cut into a grid shows it. The smooth blend breaks into tiles. A rectangle is two triangles and the blending happens inside each one. The engine does this for you too. UV is set at the corners and blended, which is why it is a smooth ramp. The color from vertex is passed on the same way and multiplied by the picture. So light the folds in vertex work out how the cloth slopes and pass it as a varying. In fragment, dark in the cloth where it slopes away. Now the folds read as folds. There is a shorter way, the color already travels from vertex to fragment multiplied by the picture. Darken it in vertex and you need no varying and no fragment function. Same picture, pixel for pixel with the tiles back. One limit of varying is only as detailed as your corners. Six stripes computed in vertex vanish on four corners because both ends compute the same number. Computed in fragment at every pixel the stripes are there. Some mistakes compile and still look wrong. First in 2D a quad mesh draws upside down. Give it size a negative y and it stands up matching a sprite beside it. Second the word vertex changes meaning. In vertex it is the corners position in the node's own pixels. In fragment it is the pixels position on the screen. Same word, different numbers. Move the node and you see it. Colored from the corner position, the gradient travels with the sprite. Colored from the pixels screen position, the sprite slides across a gradient that stays where it is. Third a polygon node only has uv when it has a texture. A texture is an image a shader can read. Without one the address is zero everywhere and a wave multiplied by it never starts. Fourth of vertex shader changes the drawing, not the node. This one draws the sprite 200 pixels away. The node's rectangle and its collision shapes stay exactly where they were. We checked both. Last when nothing waves count the vertices. Ask a mesh for its vertex array and print the size here 297. A polygon has one per point. A plane sprite has four and four cannot bend. Let's recap with Claude. Vertex runs once for each corner and decides where it goes. A shape can only bend where it has vertices. Cut a mesh until each wave has eight pieces or more. A flag is a sign of the address in the time pinned at one end with dials. A varying carries a value from the corners to every pixel. When it looks wrong count the vertices and check what each position is measured from. Your turn pays this into Claude right a go-do canvas item shader for a waving banner. Draw it with a mesh instance 2D in a quad mesh cut 31 by 7. In vertex move the corners up and down with a sign of the address across in the time. Make amplitude, frequency, and speed float uniforms and pin the left edge. Then tell me how many vertices the mesh has. Then check two things yourself. Does the left edge stay still while the rest ripples? And does the vertex count match 33 by 9? Vertex shaders at Nick Bear Brown.
