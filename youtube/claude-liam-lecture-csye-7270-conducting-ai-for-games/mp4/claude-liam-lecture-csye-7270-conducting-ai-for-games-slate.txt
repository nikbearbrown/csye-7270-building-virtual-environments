In CSY-E72700 and AI writes the code for your games, the shortcut is to think that leaves nothing for you to do. This film is about the opposite, what is left, and why it is the whole job. Five terms, conducting means directing an AI's work, choosing the goal, hearing what is off, and deciding what goes out. A coding agent is an AI that reads your project, edits it, and runs commands, to verify as to check an answer against the real thing, not against the AI's say so. A receipt is a saved record of what a check found, and play field is whether a game feels right in the hands, which only a person can judge. In this course, the AI writes the code, Claude code, or Codex reads your project, edits it, runs it, and reports back. You do not type the game. That sounds like less work. It is different work, and it is the work this course teaches. Picture an orchestra where every player has just got better. Every instrument improved, and nobody is conducting. Each player is brilliant. Nobody is holding the whole performance. The podium is empty. That is a game built by an AI with nobody directing. Each script can be fluent. Each scene can look right, and the game can still be wrong, because nobody held the whole thing. So the question for this course is not, can the AI write the code? It can. The question is what is left for you, and how to do it well. Start with an asymmetry, making an answer, and checking an answer are different jobs. The AI made the first one cheap. It did not make the second one cheap. And it gets harder, not easier. The faster the AI makes things, the more there is to check. The channel for making gets wider. The gate for checking stays person-sized. Be careful with that claim. It is an argument with limits, not a law. A tool that runs your game and measures it does check something. But a person chose that check, and a person decides whether the result is what was meant. Now the split as this course uses it. The AI does the typing, scenes, scripts, placeholder art, repeated variants, and tests from a clear description. You do the deciding. You decide what the game is for and who it is for. An AI will build any version you hand it flawlessly, including the wrong one. You hear that something is wrong before any check says so. A jump that cannot clear the gap, an enemy that cannot be dodged. A number that is too perfect. You choose which checks matter, and you look at the real running game, not the report about it. You judge whether it is fun, whether it is fair, whether it means what you intended, taste, and intent are yours. And you sign what ships, the AI faces no consequence. You do. One honest note, these ideas come from teaching people to supervise AI in medicine, law, and software. Games are where this course applies them. That application is ours, and you will be testing it. So here is a real record. The course uses a small platform called Walker Jumpman, and AI built it in a day from one request build a simple level. Its first run of 25 automatic checks failed for, dead on respawn. Restart counted as a death, a route with no safe landing. The AI fixed the game, and the next run passed all 25, 71 seconds later. Look at how the record says it did it. The jump tuning and the acceptance tolerances were not weakened. The tests stayed hard, the game changed. Then came a second step. Its author wanted the player to be a coding agent. Drawing is clawed, the clawed mascot. One changed, not the whole game. In its change brief, the AI named three ways it could go wrong. The wide body might overhang the collision box. A jump animation on a jumping body might make two jump arcs. And 18 animations might be mistaken for 18 abilities. Then it changed the drawing and the animation choice only. The physics, the tuning, the level, and the hazards were left alone. A separate test compared 1,944 numbers against an independent reference. The first prediction held up, the arm stick out past the left edge of the world. The record does not hide it. It leaves it open for a person to judge and play. Now the part that matters for this course. The machine checks were not always right. One recording of the gallery came with a receipt that said pass. It held 343 frames. The plan needed 540. The AI noticed, rejected the recording, kept it as evidence, and changed its method to require 600 frames. Another recording ended with exit code 0, the code that means success. It had no completion receipt and only 416 of 600 frames. In the record's own words, exit code 0 did not establish success. Even careful wording can outrun its check. The record says the physics function is byte identical. True. It compares one function. The same file changed by 33 lines. That is the asymmetry in a real project. A success statement records what was said. The state records what actually changed. You read the state. And these records were written by the AI itself. They are candid, but look for the human finding in them. There is none. The human play test is still marked pending. Eight design questions are still open, each with a person's name on it. That is not a flaw to hide. It is the point. The machine can tell you 100 things passed, only a person can say whether it is fun. So here is how this course runs. You direct clawed code or codex to build real games in godo, an engine you will meet in the next film. You start from a brief you write, you ask for one change at a time. You predict how it can fail before it is built and you choose the check. You keep a log what the AI did, what you decided, what you doubted. That log is the evidence of your work.
