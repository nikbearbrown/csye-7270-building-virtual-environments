### 2026-10-06 — Rebuilding the slice into a game: reading my own Assignment 1 to find out what was missing

- **Date and what I was working on:** 2026-10-06, late — turning a scene you can walk around into something that plays like a game.
- **I tried / expected:** I expected the slice to be close to finished once the art and music were in. Playing it, it was not: it looked like a picture with a character sliding across it.
- **What happened:** Three complaints of mine drove three separate rebuilds, and the last of them turned out to be the most important.

  **First, the rotation.** The flip turned the whole room 180 degrees around the player. I did not like it. Claude had argued the rotation was the signature image; I said it looked like a picture being spun. I told it to look at how Assignment 1 did it rather than keep guessing.

  **Second, the wrong character in the wrong world.** The ghost appeared in the upright world and the living boy appeared upside down, which is backwards from what the game is about.

  **Third, it was not a level.** One room, one flat floor, one platform. Assignment 1 is 4,980 pixels wide with dozens of platforms, hidden floors, phantom floors, hazards, feathers and story logs. Ours had none of that shape.

  Reading the Assignment 1 source settled all three at once. That game **never rotates the camera.** Flipping reverses gravity so the player falls upward and stands on ceilings, the palette swaps light to dark, and different geometry becomes real — hidden floors appear while inverted, phantom floors stop existing. The picture does not spin; the world changes what it is showing. That is why it felt smooth.
- **What I did:** I had the whole flip rebuilt on that model. The camera is now fixed and the two rooms cross-dissolve into each other over 0.35 seconds while gravity reverses. I also had the two states swapped to the mapping the story actually wants: **normal is the comfortable lie** — he looks like a living boy in the warm remembered bedroom, which is how he still sees himself — and **inverted is the truth**, where he becomes the ghost and the room becomes the stripped, boxed-up one the new family moved into. That is Assignment 1's own thesis applied to this game: the world shows you what you expect, so turn it over to see what is there. Then I had a real level built: three room-widths wide with a camera that follows, ten platforms and two blockers grouped by world, so furniture the memory has is solid only while normal and clutter the emptied house has is solid only while inverted. The blockers are the design: the memory world is blocked at floor level, the ceiling route is blocked further along, so the only way through is to keep turning the world over. Finally I said the movement looked like teleporting, which it did — and the cause was real.
- **What Claude or another person contributed:** Claude read my Assignment 1 source and reported back what it actually does, rather than defending its own earlier design; rebuilt the flip as gravity inversion with a cross-dissolve; generated the level geometry and wired the per-world platform switching; and diagnosed the movement problem. That diagnosis had two parts, both genuine bugs: the walk cycle only ran in the normal state, so as the ghost he slid along with his legs frozen, and the stride was 62 pixels per frame, which at full speed is fewer than five frames a second and stutters. The cycle now runs in both worlds, with the ghost using the same frames drained and translucent, and the stride is 30 pixels, about ten frames a second. Claude also fixed three faults it found while verifying rather than by being told: a collision capsule that was teleported into the floor on the first flip and squeezed the player out through the bottom of the room, a blocker placed on the floor when the route it was meant to block runs along the ceiling, and a probe that reported a failure caused by its own sequencing rather than by the game. ### 2026-10-07 — Playing it myself, and nine things that were wrong

- **Date and what I was working on:** 2026-10-07 — the first real playtests with my own hands on the controls, and the round of fixes that came out of them.
- **I tried / expected:** I expected to be checking polish. Instead almost everything I noticed was a genuine fault, and several of them were things no amount of automated checking would ever have surfaced.
- **What happened:** The character turned pale the moment he moved. He changed size and seemed to stand in a different place when he stopped. He shrank while jumping. Only the jump sound could be heard. The platforms were pointless — I could walk the whole level on flat ground and nothing I did mattered. The glowing thing meant nothing to me. And I did not know what I was supposed to be doing, which is the one that matters most.
- **What I did:** I reported each of these plainly and pushed back when the answer was not good enough. On the "I don't know what to do" problem I said explicitly that I did not want text added — a game that has to explain itself in words has failed at being a game. On the platforms I said I wanted the player to die if they did not jump, because without consequence there is no game. And I asked for obstacles that stop you in one world and can only be seen or passed from the other, tied to the story rather than invented.
- **What Claude or another person contributed:** Claude measured rather than guessed on every one. The pale character was the walk and jump sheets being graded 30 percent brighter and much cooler than the idle, fixed by matching per channel. The size and position change when stopping was the idle textures never having been rebuilt when the frame sets were unified. The shrinking jump came from three different ways of measuring the body all being wrong — height fails across poses, shoulder width fails when the arms are raised, and head width fails on the rising pose because the topmost pixels there are his raised hands; body area is what finally worked. The silent sounds were the score covering them and three of the six only firing at a music box I had little chance of reaching, fixed by raising the levels and ducking the music under every sound. For the guidance problem Claude moved the music box into the first room, visible from the spawn and out of reach from the floor, lit it permanently, and gave it a call that plays its own chime every few seconds and quietens with distance — a music box playing by itself is both what a haunting sounds like and all the instruction the slice needs. For the obstacles it proposed the answer the story already had: the memory is full of his things and the truth is empty, so his bookshelf blocks the lie and is absent from the truth, while the new family's boxes block the truth and are absent from the memory. Both props were cut out of the room paintings themselves rather than generated, so they match exactly. Claude also tried gaps in the ceiling first and abandoned them, because under reversed gravity crossing one means jumping downward, which no player will intuit; and its first gap width left a 35 pixel window to take off in against a jump covering 275 pixels, which it widened to a full body length of margin after measuring.
- **What I understand now / still do not understand:** Playing it is not the same as verifying it, and almost nothing I found today would have shown up in an automated check — they all passed the whole time. I also understand the difference between an obstacle and a trap: the floor gaps were invisible at first, which made them unfair rather than difficult, and they only became a real obstacle once you could see them. Still unresolved: whether the light now reads as a music box rather than an abstract glow, which only another playtest will tell me.
