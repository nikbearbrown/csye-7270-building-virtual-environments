# FRICTIONAL — Assignment 2

## How this log works

I set the working method on the first day and kept to it: **Claude proposes, I attack, and whatever survives is the game.** I asked it explicitly to argue back rather than agree, because an assistant that agrees with everything is worth nothing on a project I have to defend myself. Most of what is good in HOUSEGHOST exists because one of us refused the other's first answer.

The entries below are dated and in order, written as the work happened. Nothing has been rewritten after the fact; where a decision was reversed later, the reversal is its own entry rather than an edit to the original. The table that follows is an index of the decisions that actually changed the game, and who moved whom.

### 2026-10-06 — Building the slice: four things the engine told me I had got wrong

- **Date and what I was working on:** 2026-10-06 — assembling the playable slice around the art, and then making it feel like a game rather than a scene.
- **I tried / expected:** I expected the hard part to be the art and the easy part to be dropping it into Godot. The reverse was true: the art was settled, and the engine kept exposing assumptions that only look wrong once something is actually running.
- **What happened:** Four separate failures, each found by running the thing rather than by reading the code. (1) The first build showed a black window. The floor used a shape whose distance is measured along its normal, which points up, so the floor materialised at y = −940 and threw the player off the top of the screen; the room had been drawing correctly the whole time around a player who was not there. (2) The automated sound check failed two of five assertions on its first run. One was a real defect — the contact cooldown only guarded the input path, so the function itself could still fire twice if called directly — and one was a flaw in the check, which measured waits in frames when headless frames are not real time. (3) A probe showed the music box could never be touched by actually playing: it sat at the room's vertical centre while the player walks four hundred pixels below it, so the areas never overlapped. (4) Drawing the required collision overlay exposed that the capsule, specified as 40 × 80 px before any art existed, covers only the shins of the 320 px sprite that was eventually made.
- **What I did:** I fixed all four at the source rather than around them: an explicit rectangle floor, the cooldown moved inside the resolve function so it holds no matter who calls it, the music box moved onto the floor and out of the rotating room so it keeps its place through a flip, and the capsule resized to 52 × 250 px with the character sheet recording the correction instead of quietly rewriting the original number. I also asked for the slice to feel like a real game rather than a walkable scene. Claude argued against frame-by-frame animation on the grounds that the assignment says twice that it earns nothing, and proposed engine motion instead; I pushed back and said I wanted it to look like a real person moving, so we found the path that serves both — the walk poses were required for the character sheet anyway, so one generation of a four-frame cycle filled four of the required poses and became the animation.
- **What Claude or another person contributed:** Claude wrote the engine code, diagnosed each failure, and wrote the automated check that caught the contact defect. It also made two design calls I accepted: that the room should turn over while the boy stays upright, so the controls never reverse and he ends up walking the ceiling of the room as he remembers it; and that the walk cycle should belong to the remembered state alone, because a ghost does not walk, he drifts. ChatGPT produced the walk cycle sheet. The decision to push for real movement, and the acceptance of each fix, were mine.
- **What I understand now / still do not understand:** Writing a test that fails usefully is worth more than writing one that passes immediately — two of the five assertions failed on the first run and both failures were worth having, one in the game and one in the test itself. I also understand that a specification written before the art exists will contain numbers that turn out to be wrong, and that the honest response is to correct the implementation and date the correction, not to retrofit the document. Unresolved: the ghost reads faint against the grey upright room, which was predicted in CHANGE-BRIEF as a failure case and is now visible in the captured frames; the planned fix is a rim light on the sprite rather than a change to the room, and it has not been done yet.
- **Evidence and next step:** Evidence: evidence/01 to 08 captured in engine, including the walk cycle strip and the player standing on the boxes; the automated check at godot/tests/test_sound_triggers.gd, five assertions, run with `godot --headless --path godot --script tests/test_sound_triggers.gd`; asset-log rows CHAR-WALK-01..04 and UI-GLOW; the commits of 2026-10-06. ### 2026-10-06 — Music: two worlds, two tracks, and a loop that stopped the moment I made it loop

- **Date and what I was working on:** 2026-10-06 — generating and fitting the music.
- **I tried / expected:** I expected to generate one lullaby for the remembered world, drop it in, and move on.
- **What happened:** Three things went wrong before anything went right. The first prompt went into Suno's Lyrics field instead of the Styles field, so it sang my production notes back to me. The first usable results at 70 BPM were too slow to sit under a game the player walks and jumps through — fine for a cutscene, wrong for play. And once a track was in the engine, enabling Godot's loop mode stopped playback instantly, because `loop_end` defaults to zero and I had quietly created a zero-length loop.
- **What I did:** I raised the tempo to 100 BPM and asked for a driving sixteenth-note arpeggio so the dread comes from repetition rather than from emptiness. Then I decided the upright world should not be silent after all — hearing it built, the silence read as unfinished rather than tense — so I generated a second track and gave each world its own. ### 2026-10-07 — What a contrast check measures, and what it was actually measuring

- **Date and what I was working on:** 2026-10-07 — the film's final visual gate.
- **I tried / expected:** I had declared the gameplay beats as full-bleed and the building cards as sparse, each with a written reason, and expected the gate to clear.
- **What happened:** It did clear structurally — every blocker went. What remained was fourteen findings all saying the same thing: the label burned onto the gameplay clips was unreadable, separation around 0.05 against a floor of 0.3. That reading was wrong, and the reason it was wrong was mine. I had drawn the region I asked the gate to measure by estimating where the label sat, and the box came out wider than the label. So the check was averaging a bright white label together with the dark room beside it and reporting the average.
- **What I did:** Stopped guessing at the coordinates and measured them.
- **What Claude or another person contributed:** Claude sampled frames from all seven gameplay beats, found the label plate's true extent in each, and tested candidate boxes against every one. A correctly sized region measures 0.944 separation at its worst — comfortably legible. The declaration now carries that number and says the earlier box was wider than the label, so the correction is visible to anyone reading it rather than buried.
- **What I understand now / still do not understand:** A measurement is only as good as the region you point it at, and a confident wrong number looks exactly like a confident right one. The gate was not wrong about what it measured; it was measuring what I told it to. Unresolved: the gate's redundancy advisory still notes four beats whose narration recites its card rather than discussing it, and I accepted that rather than re-voicing them.
