An engineer robot whose arm is an instrument for designing, connecting to, and watching agents. It never does the work itself.
A jig is a workshop tool that holds a piece in place and guides other tools, so the work comes out right every time. Engineers don’t cut with a jig; they build a jig so the cutting goes well. That is the whole character. Jig doesn’t do the work. Jig designs the setup that lets other machines do it well.
Jig is tall and lean, built more like a surveyor than a soldier, with a slight forward stoop like someone who spends a lot of time bent over a drafting table. The frame is graphite grey with cream panels and safety-orange accents at the joints: the colors of a well-kept workshop. The legs are sturdy and flat-footed with a wide stance. Jig is built to stand still and watch, not to run.
The chest plate is scored with a faint measuring grid. Around the waist is a belt of slim pockets holding blank metal tags, which Jig stamps and hands to the agents it builds (see Draft mode).
The head is a compact rounded rectangle with no face in the usual sense. Instead of eyes, a horizontal glass vial runs across the front with a single bright bubble inside, like a spirit level. The bubble drifts toward whatever Jig is paying attention to. When every agent is running clean, it settles dead center. An off-center, jittering bubble means something needs attention. That gives Jig a readable expression without a face.
The left arm is an ordinary, dexterous hand. It drafts, points, gestures, and stamps tags. It never holds a tool.
The right arm is the instrument. Everything below the elbow is built around three verbs: tell, listen, design.
It looks like a hand, slightly heavier than the left. Along the outer edge of the forearm runs a long dark glass panel, dormant but faintly pulsing. At the elbow sits a cylindrical housing ringed with six small spool caps.
When Jig connects, the change is slow and fully visible. The fingers fold back along the back of the hand one at a time, the palm splits along its centerline, and a connector slides out from the wrist core. The hand doesn’t vanish; it opens to reveal what’s inside.
The silhouette should always tell you the mode. An open hand means Jig is just Jig. An extended connector means Jig is connected to something.
The connector looks like a heavy-duty audio plug: a smooth metal shaft divided into three bands by dark insulating rings. Each band is a channel and lights up when it’s in use.
Sleeve: trust. The band nearest the wrist is the handshake. Before anything flows, the sleeve has to lock. The agent identifies itself and Jig confirms it is the agent Jig designed. If the sleeve doesn’t lock, nothing else lights.
Ring: listen. The middle band carries incoming status, results, and feedback. It glows cool white while data is coming back.
Tip: tell. The tip carries outgoing specifications, instructions, and adjustments. It glows warm amber while Jig is sending.
The lighting sequence is the lesson: trust first, then tell, then listen. Listening never stops while the jack is connected.
At the base of the jack sits a rotating metal collar shaped like a camera iris, and every piece of incoming data passes through it. It has three positions:
- Open: data passes straight through.
- Filtered: the iris half-closes and data slows through a visible mesh.
- Sealed: the iris shuts and incoming data is held in quarantine.
Quarantined data appears on the forearm screen inside a hatched box. Jig can read it, but it can’t flow into any agent, or into Jig’s own decisions, until Jig clears it.
The collar enforces the arm’s most important rule: what comes back through the jack is information, never orders. An agent’s report can tell Jig what happened. It cannot tell Jig what to do. This is the arm’s defense against a hijacked agent.
The forearm screen runs from wrist to elbow. Jig reads it by raising the forearm level, like checking a watch. That is Jig’s signature pose.
The screen has two views.
Fleet view is the default while Jig is supervising several agents. Each connected agent gets a horizontal lane. Tick marks along the lane mark the phases of its job, and a small marker shows where the agent is right now. Gates are drawn as vertical bars across the lanes; when an agent reaches a gate, it stops and pulses until Jig clears it. A lane that goes grey means feedback from that agent has stopped. When an agent does something outside its expected pattern, a small notch flashes on its lane.
Focus view appears when the jack is plugged into a single agent. The screen shows that agent’s design next to its live state: its tag, what it takes in and hands back, its gates, and its boundary. This is where design happens. Jig edits the spec with left-hand gestures on the glass, and the changes flow out through the tip.
The screen deliberately never shows raw logs. If Jig is staring at scrolling text, something has already gone wrong.
The six spools at the elbow are for supervising many agents at once. In conduct mode, thin cables pay out from the spools, one to each agent. A tether carries a lighter version of the jack’s channels: enough to tell and listen, but not enough for deep design work. For that, Jig walks over and plugs in the jack.
Six is a hard limit. The arm physically cannot supervise more. A seventh agent forces a design decision: merge two jobs into one, or build a sub-orchestrator that takes a single tether and runs agents of its own.
Tension in the tethers is a visual language anyone can read:
- Slack: the agent is idle or waiting. Slack plus a grey lane means it’s lost.
- Steady, light tension: the agent is working normally.
- Taut: the agent is straining against its boundary, trying to do more than it was designed for. The spool brake clicks and holds it at the edge.
- Crossed or tangled: two agents are interfering with each other. This is the visible cost of jobs that weren’t separated cleanly.
To design a new agent or rebuild a broken one, Jig plugs the jack into the agent’s core port, seals the collar (nothing from a broken agent is trusted), and opens focus view.
The first act of every design is the tag. Jig takes a blank tag from the belt and stamps the agent’s single purpose on it in one line. A red hairline along the tag’s bottom edge marks the agent’s boundary. Every agent Jig builds wears its tag where Jig can see it. An agent without a tag isn’t one of Jig’s.
It can’t cut, weld, lift, or shoot. If something physical needs to happen in the world, an agent does it. This is the character’s defining constraint: the arm amplifies judgment, not force.
The arm also follows two behavioral rules. When the screen goes dark, Jig stops sending instructions, because it never flies blind. When the sleeve won’t lock, the tip doesn’t light, because Jig never instructs an agent it can’t identify.
Two connection systems may be one too many. The jack-plus-tethers split is accurate to how orchestration works, since deep design is different from broad supervision. But for a physical representation meant to teach, it adds complexity. A simpler alternative is for the jack itself to split into tethers, so there is one connector that branches.
The color budget is tight. Amber, white, red, grey lanes, and quarantine hatching are already a lot. Every state should also be readable by shape or motion (pulse, hatch, slack, notch), so nothing depends on color alone.
The left hand acts directly. It drafts and stamps, which means the orchestrator does touch something. That is correct: design is direct work on specifications, not on the world. Make that distinction explicit, or the left hand will read as a loophole in the “never does the work” rule.
